Add Ann Arbor's animals, starting with squirrels and the river's birds. One rule above everything: animals are never struck, hurt or knocked down by anything, whether a car, a bike, the helicopter, the kayak or the player falling. They aren't obstacles, aren't in the ragdoll or knock-down code and never make a thud. Put each one into the game as it's done, and keep the frame rate and memory where they are (check both before and after each; the game already runs close to the browser's memory limit on lamp.dev).

Every animal is a high-resolution model that holds up close, in walking view and first person: a real body shape with fur or feathers (shaded fur and feather textures, not flat color), eyes, ears, paws and tail for a squirrel, proper wings that fold and spread for the birds, and a skeleton so each walks, hops, swims and flies with real motion, not a sliding or bobbing block. Use well-made models with a license that allows this (CC0 or CC BY, credited in credits.json), or build them in code to that same standard. Look at each one up close in daylight before calling it done. To keep memory in check, share one model and texture set per species across every animal of that kind, and swap to a simpler version only once it's far enough that nobody could tell.

1. Squirrels: the campus's famous bold squirrels, gray, fox-red and some black, on the Diag, the campus lawns, the parks and the street trees' trunks and lower branches. They hop in bursts, stop, sit up, flick their tails, chase each other round a trunk and come near anyone standing still on foot. Only the ones near the player are drawn.
2. Getting away: anything moving toward an animal (a car, bike or the player running) sends it off well before it gets there. Squirrels bolt to the nearest tree and up it, birds take off, deer bound into the woods. Driving across the Diag should scatter every squirrel on it, which is the fun part. If something still reaches one (it appeared right in front of the player, say), it leaps clear or quietly vanishes. It's never touched.
3. The river: mallards and Canada geese on the Huron, Argo Pond and Gallup Park's water, paddling and dabbling, flying off low over the water when the kayak gets close. Geese also graze Gallup's lawns and honk and hiss at someone on foot who walks right up to them, then waddle off. A great blue heron stands in the shallows below the Cascades and lifts off slowly when anyone comes near.
4. The sky and the Arb: a red-tailed hawk or two circling over the Arb and the Huron in daylight, and a few white-tailed deer at the Arb's woods' edges at dawn and dusk who look up, freeze, then bound off when someone comes near. Nothing flies into the helicopter: birds steer clear of it.
5. Sound: squirrel chatter, quacks, goose honks and wingbeats on takeoff, a hawk's cry now and then, all in the city's sound and quiet at night (the animals keep to day and dusk too: squirrels asleep after dark, birds roosting).

Keep APP.md up to date with where the animals live in the code and the never-struck rule, so later changes keep it.
